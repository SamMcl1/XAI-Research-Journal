## What we discussed / decided

Team meeting to plan the short paper. We decided the paper should carry the argument from our presentation further rather than repeat it. We worked through a set of questions that will shape our first draft of our paper.

**Decided:**
- **Topic and scope:** a critical analysis of SHAP explanations for reviewing false-positive cheating flags, with XGuardian as the main case study.
- **Research question:** *Can SHAP-based explanations be expected to help reviewers recognise false-positive cheating flags, and what evidence currently supports that expectation?*
- **Title:** *Explaining the Flag, Not the Player: A Critical Analysis of SHAP Explanations for Reviewing False Positives in AI Anti-Cheat.*

## Questions we worked through

### 1. Do we need to train our own model?
On 2 October we asked whether we could make a meaningful argument without building a model. We decided **no new model**: the paper is a structured case study of XGuardian's paper and released code. Training and evaluating a sequence model properly would not fit in the time left. The released code also already let us check details the paper's prose leaves vague. We state this openly in the limitations rather than hiding it.

### 2. What exactly is being explained?
Andy's code check on 5 October found that the final random forest receives only one input by default: `original_prediction_avg`. We worked through what that means for TreeSHAP. With one feature, the attribution must equal the score minus the baseline, so the match-level explanation adds no information beyond the score itself. All the useful explanatory content sits in the per-kill GradientExplainer trajectories. This became one of our main findings, and we linked it to Rudin's argument for interpretable models in high-stakes decisions.

### 3. Do the headline metrics tell us about innocent players?
Two problems came up:
- XGuardian's reported "weighted FPR" (5.9%) is calculated from predicted-class columns. That makes it the complement of weighted precision, not the usual rate of legitimate players flagged.
- Even a correctly calculated FPR doesn't describe what a reviewer sees. We worked an illustrative example: if 5% of players cheat, then with 90.7% recall and a 4.1% FPR, roughly 46% of flags would be innocent. We agreed to label the 5% clearly as an assumption, not a study figure.

The Minecraft paper's ~99.7% F1 on 13 players has the same gap: no false-positive rate is reported.


### 4. Is there evidence that explanations actually help reviewers?
We looked at the evidence presented in the slides again:
- **XGuardian user study:** 4 reviewers, 6 cases, different innocent cases in each condition. It supports the narrow claim that explanations did not bias reviewers into accepting wrong flags, but it cannot isolate a SHAP effect.
- **Weerts et al. (2019):** a controlled test showed no significant benefit (59% vs 61%, p = 0.346), on a non-gaming task.

We concluded the evidence is anecdotal. We used Doshi-Velez & Kim's distinction between human-grounded and application-grounded evaluation to explain why.


### 5. Can a correct explanation still mislead?
We reused the footsteps example from slide 11. An aim-only model never hears the sound cue, so a perfectly faithful explanation still highlights a legitimate pre-aim as suspicious. We linked this to the week 5 counterfactuals lecture: people ask contrastive questions ("why cheating rather than skill?") that feature attribution over aim kinematics cannot answer. We also flagged the tick index as an input whose attributions describe *when* something happened, not *what* the player did.


### 6. Should we compare SHAP with LIME?
We considered a SHAP vs LIME comparison as the paper's core (it would have matched the "technique comparison" example in the brief). **Rejected as the main topic.** XGuardian never uses LIME, and without running both ourselves we would only be speculating. We kept a shorter discussion instead:
- LIME perturbs features independently, which produces physically impossible aim trajectories.
- SHAP is not automatically stable either: GradientExplainer samples, temporal squeezing averages overlapping estimates, and the background set affects the baseline.

This connects to the week 5 LIME exercise where results changed without a fixed `random_state`.


### 7. What would actually answer our research question?
We agreed the paper should end with something constructive, not just criticism. We expanded the slide 12 proposal into a randomised, counterbalanced reviewer study with three arms: no explanation, real SHAP explanation, and randomly permuted explanation. The third arm tests automation bias. The primary outcome is innocent players judged as cheating, with missed cheaters as a secondary outcome. That way, a "benefit" can't come simply from reviewers rejecting more flags.


### 8. What do we leave out?
We agreed to state these explicitly in the limitations:
- No experiment or reproduction of our own.
- Minecraft paper read from the abstract only.
- FPR definition critiqued but not recomputed.
- Visual cheats, adversarial evasion and the legal side of appeals are out of scope.
- Player trust is not covered. This corrects our 2 October claim that XGuardian quantified player trust: its study is about reviewer decisions.


## Why
The rubric rewards critical analysis and visible group reasoning over literature summary. Each question above turns something we learned for the presentation into an argument the paper makes. The presentation established "useful support, limited proof". The paper explains *why* the proof is limited and *what would fix it*.

## Sources reviewed (new for the paper)
- Doshi-Velez & Kim (2017), *Towards a Rigorous Science of Interpretable Machine Learning*. Course reading, used for the evaluation framing.
- Rudin (2019), *Stop Explaining Black Box Machine Learning Models for High Stakes Decisions*. Supports the point in question 2.
- Kumar et al. (2020), *Problems with Shapley-value-based explanations as feature importance measures*. Supports reading SHAP as describing the model, not the world.
- Slack et al. (2020), *Fooling LIME and SHAP*. Supports the point that both explainers can be manipulated.
- Parasuraman & Manzey (2010), *Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation*. Supports the automation bias arm of the proposed study.
- Miller (2019), *Explanation in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Social Sciences*. Supports contrastive explanations (week 5 counterfactuals lecture).
- Lundberg et al. (2020), TreeSHAP, and Erion et al. (2021), expected gradients. Technical background for the two explainers.

### Considered and rejected
- **YOLO aimbot papers (2023, 2024):** offensive tools with no explanation component, and image-based, which is outside our scope.
- **GAN-Aimbots (2022):** relevant to adversarial evasion, which we have explicitly scoped out. It was also the duplicate link flagged on 24 September.
- **Full SHAP vs LIME benchmark:** see question 6.
